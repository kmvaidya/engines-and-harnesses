# Round 4: what makes writing read as AI-generated, and how to fix it

Round 4 file, gathered 2026-11-04 to back the house writing rules for both
decks (see the "not AI" feedback from August: cut em dashes, aphorism closers
and staccato fragments; gloss jargon). Three research tracks ran in parallel:
an editorial catalog of tells, the academic studies, and remedies. Where two
tracks found the same fact independently, that is noted as "two tracks".

Tags, same as rounds 2 and 3:

- **[P]** confirmed against the primary source (the paper, the publisher's
  page, the vendor's docs) on 2026-11-04
- **[P\*]** primary source fetched on 2026-11-04, but the quote came back
  through a page summariser; check the wording before putting it on a slide
- **[S]** secondary source (news write-up, mirror, search snippet, citing paper)
- **[E]** extracted from a named source, not cross-checked
- **[R]** refuted or a common misquotation; recorded so nobody reuses it
- **[O]** our recommendation, not sourced

## 1. The short version

1. **Tells are signs, not proof.** Wikipedia's own catalog says "This list is
   descriptive, not prescriptive" and "Please do not merely treat these signs
   as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder." [P]
2. **Most people can't tell, but heavy AI users can.** People who rarely use
   ChatGPT scored near chance. Five people who use it often for writing were
   right 92.7% of the time on their own, and their majority vote missed 1
   article in 300 (Russell et al., ACL 2025). [P]
3. **Word lists go stale.** "Delve" rose 68-fold in PubMed abstracts and then
   fell below its pre-ChatGPT baseline in podcasts once it became a meme. Each
   model generation brings its own words: GPT-6 says "dependable" and
   "practical", Claude Opus 5.5 says "this matters". [P]
4. **Structure gives text away after the words are fixed.** Experts still
   caught 100% of "humanized" articles. They went by sentence templates,
   uniform paragraphs, generic openings and neat upbeat conclusions. [P]
5. **The deeper signal is the absence of specifics.** Generic names (60-70%
   of AI articles used "Emily" or "Sarah"), vague attributions ("experts
   argue"), no first-hand detail, and the most typical idea instead of a
   particular one. [P]
6. **The em dash flipped in 2026.** GPT-4.1 and GPT-5 used about twice the
   human rate. GPT-6, Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 5.5 now use far fewer
   than humans. Having no dashes at all is becoming a tell in its own right. [P]
7. **Detectors and readers want different things.** Detectors flag plain,
   predictable wording and miss ornate wording. Plain writing for a person is
   still the right target; ignore detector scores. [P]
8. **The fixes are old advice.** Orwell, the US federal plain-language
   guidelines, GOV.UK and Pinker already cover nearly every tell. The newer
   finding is that defining jargon does not undo the damage it does
   (Shulman et al. 2020); removing or rewriting it works better. [P]

## 2. How far to trust any list of tells

- [P] Wikipedia: "Not all text featuring these indicators is AI-generated";
  "Many elements of AI writing can be found in editorials, blogs, or fan
  fiction." The page is an advice page, not a policy; last edited
  2026-11-04, oldid 1383697236.
- [P] Wikipedia lists **ineffective indicators**: perfect grammar, transition
  words in general ("only a few transition words and phrases are known to be
  overused by AI"), and missing citations ("citations are fairly common now in
  AI-generated text").
- [P] Wikipedia on model differences: "what is typical for GPT-5 is not
  necessarily characteristic of GPT-4 or Gemini"; "Gemini and Claude responses
  tend to be more concise than responses from ChatGPT and Grok."
- [P] Graphite (Sep 16, 2026): "65% of tells are unique to one model family,"
  and 55-72% are unique to a single version. The total count of tells is
  roughly steady, because labs remove the famous ones and new ones appear.
  Graphite is a marketing firm; its data is CC BY 4.0 and not peer-reviewed.
  A critic (Pebblous [S]) notes the em-dash figures come from one fixed
  prompt.
- [P] Russell et al.: 31% of the experts' false positives were triggered by
  human text that happened to contain "delve" or "crucial". One tell word is
  weak evidence; judge the pattern.
- [P*] A veteran speechwriter's own human-written speeches got 24 to 28
  "tells" from a Claude detection prompt, mostly triads, em dashes and
  transitions (Pro Rhetoric). Speechwriting devices are human rhetoric that
  models learned.

## 3. The catalog of tells

### 3a. Word choice

**Vocabulary by era** (Wikipedia [P], cross-checked against the studies in §6):

| Era | Overused words |
|---|---|
| 2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4) | delve, tapestry, testament, underscore, pivotal, intricate/intricacies, meticulously, garner, vibrant, valuable, enduring, crucial |
| Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o) | align with, enhance, fostering, highlighting, showcasing, emphasizing |
| Mid-2025 to mid-2026 (GPT-5) | emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing, plus notability and connection words |
| Mid-2026 on (GPT-6) | challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally |

- [P] Wikipedia: a batch of these words in one post-2022 edit is "one of the
  strongest tells for AI use."
- [P] Measured lists from the studies: Liang et al. 2024 peer reviews
  (commendable, innovative, meticulous, intricate, notable, versatile,
  noteworthy, invaluable, pivotal); Russell et al. Table 12 (tapestry,
  testament, realm, landscape, multifaceted, nuance, journey; delve, elevate,
  embrace, empower, foster, harness, navigate, resonate, underscore; crucial,
  pivotal, vibrant, seamless, transformative, profound, robust); Reinhart et
  al. GPT-4o vs humans (camaraderie 162x, tapestry 155x, intricate 119x,
  underscore 107x, amidst 100x, palpable 95x).
- [P] **Avoiding plain "is"**: "serves as," "stands as," "marks,"
  "represents," "boasts," "features." A human wrote "is LAAA's exhibition
  arm"; the AI version said "serves as LAAA's exhibition space" (Wikipedia).
- [P] **Vague connection words**: "in connection with," "associated with,"
  "widely associated." Wikipedia counts this among the most common signs as
  of October 2026.
- [P] **Stock phrases**: "it's important to note," "is a testament," "crucial
  role in," "paving the way," "shed light on," "in a world where" (Pangram;
  Russell et al.).
- [S] The Economist (Jul 30, 2026, via Gruber): "'and' is their most overused
  word"; "more Latinate suffixes crop up in their writing than in human
  texts."
- [P] Galpin, Anderson & Juzek (2025): spikes hit whole clusters of
  near-synonyms together. Banning one word invites its neighbour.

### 3b. Sentence structure and rhythm

- [P] **Trailing -ing clauses**: "..., highlighting/underscoring/ensuring/
  reflecting/contributing to/fostering X" (Wikipedia). Reinhart et al.
  measured present participial clauses at 2-5x the human rate in
  instruction-tuned models (GPT-4o 5.3x).
- [P] **Noun-heavy register**: nominalizations at 1.5-2x the human rate
  (GPT-4o 2.1x), "that" clauses as subject 2.6x, phrasal coordination 1.9x.
  The models keep this style "even when prompted to match the style of
  informal speech and writing" (Reinhart et al., PNAS 2025).
- [P] **Uniform paragraphs**: "very organized paragraphs that are all about
  the same length"; sentences "do not vary much in length or style"; few
  sentences start with "And" or "But" (Pangram 2025).
- [E] **Punchy fragments**: "Not a detail. A design decision." One blog
  (explainx.ai, Aug 2026, sourced from forum threads, no counts) calls this
  Claude's most common tic, with colon and semicolon pivots used in place
  of "but" or "because".
- [P] **Tradeoff-dodging**: "without requiring" 70x (GPT-6 Astra); "without
  losing" and "without sacrificing" in all three model families (Graphite).
- **Open conflict on rhythm.** Wikipedia [P] says GPT-6 era text has "much
  more period and comma usage" and sentences of 8-20 words. The Economist [S]
  says models "use fewer commas and semicolons than humans (and hardly any
  parentheses)" and "write longer sentences." They studied different models
  in different months. Do not cite either as general.

### 3c. Rhetorical moves

- [P] **Negative parallelism**, "stereotypically an 'AI sign'" (Wikipedia).
  Three forms: "not just X, but also Y"; "it's not X, it's Y"; and the
  mid-2026 reversal "Y rather than X".
  - Measured (Graphite [P]): GPT-6 Astra "the _ is not simply" 576x, "rather
    than relying" 187x; Claude Opus 5 "rather than merely" 160x, "less like a
    _ and more like" 105x; Gemini 3.1 Pro "is not just a _ it is" 153x.
  - Pangram [P]: "not just X but Y" at 1 vs 3 per 10,000 words (3x).
  - OpenAI's own GPT-6 guide tells the model to drop it (see §8a).
- [P] **Rule of three**: "LLMs overuse the rule of three," used to make
  shallow analysis look thorough (Wikipedia). Pangram measures triads at 5
  vs 19 per 10,000 words (4x).
- [P] **Announcing importance**: GPT-6 Astra "matters because" 357x;
  Claude Opus 5 "matters because" 132x, "looking ahead the" 33x; Claude Opus
  5.5 "this matters" 116x, "why _ matters" 92x, "is more than a _ it" 98x;
  Gemini 3.1 Pro "ultimately this" 78x (Graphite).
- [P] **Formula conclusions**: "Despite its... faces several challenges...,"
  "Despite these challenges," "Future Outlook" (Wikipedia). The tell is the
  rigid formula, not mentioning challenges.
- [P] **Neat, upbeat endings**: Russell et al.'s experts cited
  "repetitive and overly optimistic summaries" (13.1% of explanations). Their
  guidebook: "AI-generated text always ends with a neat conclusion."
- [P] **Humanizer skill list** (github.com/blader/humanizer): "One-line
  closers and dramatic fragments," "Sayings that sound deep," "Staged run-up
  before the point," "Arguing with no one."

### 3d. Formatting

- [P] Pangram, per 10,000 words:

| Feature | Human | AI | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Markdown overall | 8 | 90 | 12x |
| Bold | 2 | 65 | 43x |
| Bullet lists | 3 | 28 | 9x |
| Unusual Unicode (─, ≈ and similar) | 28 | 71 | 3x |

- [P] Wikipedia style signs: title case headings, bold scattered through
  prose, "inline-header vertical lists" (a bolded label then a colon on every
  bullet), emoji at the start of headings or bullets, curly quotes.
- [P] Human writers use exclamation marks more than 100 times as often as
  each of the three current model families (Graphite).
- [P] Leftovers that prove AI use outright: `contentReference[oaicite:1]`,
  `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, "[Describe the specific section...]", "2025-XX-XX",
  "Certainly! Here's a" (Wikipedia; Pangram).

### 3e. Tone

- [P] **Puffery**: "stands as," "is a testament," "a pivotal role,"
  "indelible mark," "enduring legacy." LLMs "regress to the mean": "inventor
  of the first train-coupling device" becomes "a revolutionary titan of
  industry" (Wikipedia). Wikipedia now marks the blatant promotional tone as
  historical (2023-2025).
- [P] **Hedging by era** (Wikipedia): "it's important to note" (2023-24);
  "based on available information" (2024-25); "should be treated as...
  rather than...," "does not by itself establish" (mid-2026, GPT-6 era).
  Graphite measures "does not establish" at 275x Opus 5.5's rate for GPT-6
  Astra.
- [P] **Relentless positivity**: AI "tends to be overly positive, and avoid
  criticizing" (Pangram); "AI tends to be inherently positive, attempting to
  emotionally uplift the reader, especially towards the conclusion" (Russell
  et al. guidebook).
- [P] **Mannered prose**, scored 0-100 by an LLM judge (Graphite): human
  baseline 6.65; Claude Opus 5 16.75; Claude Opus 5.5 10.57; GPT-6 Astra about
  1.2x human.
- [P] **Chatbot voice**: "I hope this helps," "Would you like...," "let me
  know" (Wikipedia).

### 3f. Content

- [P] **Vague attribution**: "Industry reports," "Observers have cited,"
  "Experts argue," "several sources" when one is cited (Wikipedia).
- [P] **Nothing specific**: "usually very vague and full of generalities";
  "tries extremely hard to avoid proper nouns"; "aggregates so many
  perspectives that it has none itself" (Pangram 2025).
- [P] **Generic names**: 63.3% of GPT-4o and 70% of Claude 3.5 articles
  contained "Emily" or "Sarah"; titles like Dr. and Prof. on everyone
  (Russell et al.). Fiction gets "Elara Voss" and "Kael" (Kriss, NYT, [S]).
- [P] **Quotes that all sound alike**: every expert source "speaks the same
  way" (22.3% of expert explanations). Humans repeat "says"; AI rotates
  "notes" and "explains" (Russell et al.).
- [P] **Telling instead of showing, over-explaining**: 19.5% of expert
  explanations (Russell et al.).
- [P] **The typical idea**: 25 models asked for "a metaphor about time"
  collapsed into two answers, "time is a river" and "time is a weaver"
  (Jiang et al., "Artificial Hivemind", NeurIPS 2025).
- [P] **Restating**: "will often repeat the prompt back to the user" and
  "often repeats itself, especially in the conclusions" (Pangram 2025).

## 4. The em dash

- [S] Sam Altman, X, Nov 13, 2025: "If you tell ChatGPT not to use em-dashes
  in your custom instructions, it finally does what it's supposed to do!"
  (via Ars Technica and TechCrunch, two tracks). Before that, users "were
  unable to get the chatbot to stop using the symbol, even when they
  specifically asked it not to" (TechCrunch).
- [S] Ars Technica: "No one knows precisely why LLMs tend to overuse em
  dashes." Theories: 19th-century scanned books (Goedecke [P]: "those books
  use ~30% more em-dashes than contemporary English prose"), Medium's
  automatic dash conversion, and raters rewarding dashes (speculation).
- [P] Freeburg, "The Last Fingerprint" (arXiv 2603.27006, preprint): told to
  avoid markdown, 12 models dropped headers and bullets but kept em dashes,
  from 0.0 (Llama) to 9.1 per 1,000 words (GPT-4.1). The habit shows up
  before RLHF (the feedback-training stage).
- [P] Graphite, em dashes per 1,000 words, human baseline 3.14:

| Model | Rate | vs human |
|---|---|---|
| GPT-4.1 | 7.13 | 2.3x |
| GPT-5 | 6.92 | 2.2x |
| Claude Opus 5 | 2.92 | about equal |
| GPT-6 Astra | 0.37 | 1/8 |
| Claude Opus 4.6 | 0.20 | 1/16 |
| Claude Opus 5.5 | 0.015 | 1/200 |
| Gemini 3.1 Pro | 0.014 | 1/230 |

- [S] The Economist (Jul 30, 2026): "Today only Claude uses more em-dashes
  than human writers"; "Humans rejoice — and start using dashes again."
  Graphite's later Opus 5.5 figure supersedes the Claude part.
- [P] Wikipedia moved em dashes to "Historical indicators (2022-September
  2026)" and says the newest models are "hard coded to not use em dashes at
  all," which makes "commas and periods... more likely now than even human
  text."
- [P] Russell et al. (early 2025) recorded experts saying AI is "usually
  grammatically perfect (also avoiding dashes and ellipses)". So the dash
  signal has pointed both ways depending on the model.
- [P] Writers' pushback: "Writers have been using me long before the advent
  of AI" (McSweeney's); Benjamin Dreyer, Random House copy chief, says
  people "should simply use dashes because they're useful" [P\*].

**For our decks [O]:** the rule against heavy em-dash use still stands,
because Kay asked for it and the decks were drafted with models that used
them. Zero dashes is not the goal. An occasional dash where it helps the
reader is fine.

## 6. What the studies show

### 6a. Excess vocabulary in scientific writing

- [P] **Kobak et al.**, *Science Advances* 11(27) eadt3813, July 2025
  (arXiv 2406.07016). Method borrowed from excess-mortality studies: project
  each word's 2024 frequency from 2021-22 and measure the gap. Delves 28x,
  underscores 13.8x, showcasing 10.7x. 2024 had 454 excess words, against
  190 at the Covid peak, and 66% were style verbs. At least 13.5% of 2024
  PubMed abstracts were processed with an LLM.
  - [R] News coverage often cites "10%" and "up to 30%". Those are the June
    2024 preprint figures. The published figures are 13.5% and 40%, and they
    are lower bounds for abstracts processed (edited counts), not "papers
    written by AI".
  - [P] Kobak: "native speakers may be better at noticing and actively
    removing unnatural style words from LLM outputs."
- [P] **Holzwarth, González-Márquez & Kobak**, arXiv 2608.10715 (Aug 2026
  preprint): with a new estimator, 89% of PubMed Central full papers in
  December 2025 show signs of LLM-assisted writing or editing; earlier 12-16%
  estimates "systematically underestimate".
- [P] **Liang et al.**, ICML 2024 (arXiv 2403.07183): 6.5-16.9% of
  *sentences* in AI conference peer reviews were substantially LLM-modified.
  "Commendable", "meticulous" and "intricate" rose 9.8x, 34.7x and 11.2x. The
  share was higher near the deadline, in reviews with low reviewer
  confidence, and in reviews without citations.
  - [R] "17% of peer reviews written by AI" is wrong twice: the figure is a
    share of sentences, and 16.9% is one venue (EMNLP), the top of the range.
- [P] **Liang et al.**, COLM 2024 (arXiv 2404.01268): computer science
  abstracts reached 17.5% by February 2024; top marker words "realm,
  intricate, showcasing, pivotal".

### 6b. Why models write like this

- [P] **Instruction tuning, not size** (Reinhart et al., PNAS 2025): base
  Llama models match human style closely; the instruction-tuned versions do
  not. "instruction tuning appears to make the model output less human, not
  more." A classifier told human from a single model's text 93-98% of the
  time.
- [P] **Feedback training, partly** (Juzek & Ward, COLING 2025): the 21 focal
  words ("delve," "showcasing," "underscores," "intricate"...) are rare in the
  training data. The tuned Llama-2-Chat finds AI-style text far less
  surprising than the base model does, which is "consistent with" RLHF.
  Their human-preference test was inconclusive, and people disliked "delve"
  specifically.
  - [R] The paper does not show "RLHF causes delve". It also found no support
    for the "Nigerian English annotators" theory from the Guardian.
- [P] **Typicality bias** (Zhang et al., "Verbalized Sampling", arXiv
  2510.01171): human raters prefer the more typical response 51.6-60.8% of
  the time, so feedback training pushes models toward the most typical
  text. Some summaries say "54-62%" [R].
- [P] **Templates from pretraining** (Shaib et al., EMNLP 2024): 76% of
  model sentence templates appear in pretraining data (35% for human text);
  they "are not overwritten during fine-tuning processes such as RLHF."
- [P] **Less diversity after feedback training**: RLHF "significantly
  reduces output diversity" (Kirk et al., ICLR 2024). Essays co-written with
  InstructGPT converged; essays written with base GPT-3 did not (Padmakumar &
  He, ICLR 2024). Stories with AI ideas were rated better but "are more
  similar to each other" (Doshi & Hauser, *Science Advances* 2024).
- [P] Some "slop" patterns appear "over 1,000x more frequently in LLM output
  than human text" (Paech et al., Antislop, arXiv 2510.15061).

### 6c. Who can spot it, and how

**Russell, Karpinska & Iyyer**, "People who frequently use ChatGPT for
writing tasks are accurate and robust detectors of AI-generated text," ACL
2025, DOI 10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.267. [P]

- 300 non-fiction articles, each human piece paired with an AI piece on the
  same title. Models: GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, o1-pro, plus paraphrased and
  "humanized" versions.
- People who rarely use LLMs: true-positive rate 56.7%, false-positive rate
  about 52%, while rating their confidence 4.03 out of 5.
- Frequent users, untrained: 92.7% true positives, 3-4% false positives.
  Majority vote of five: 1 error in 300. One expert scored 0% on humanized
  text, fooled by contractions and informality.
- Only one automatic detector (Pangram) matched them. GPTZero caught 46.7% of
  humanized text.

What the experts cited, as a share of their explanations:

| Clue | Share |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | 53.1% |
| Sentence structure | 35.9% |
| Grammar and punctuation | 24.8% |
| Originality ("safe," no surprises or humor) | 23.7% |
| Quotes (all sources sound alike) | 22.3% |
| Clarity (over-explaining, telling not showing) | 19.5% |
| Formatting | 15.0% |
| Conclusions (neat, upbeat summaries) | 13.1% |
| Formality (no filler, contractions, slang) | 12.3% |
| Names and titles | 11.7% |
| Tone (always neutral or positive) | 9.3% |

- [P] Quote: "neither paraphrasing nor humanization effectively removes all
  of these signatures."
- Caution [O]: the 1-in-300 figure is a five-person majority vote, not
  typical individual accuracy; individuals varied a lot. American English non-fiction only,
  2024-generation models.
- [P] Wikipedia also cites a German study of theses with recognition rates
  of 57% for AI texts and 64% for human texts.

### 6d. Detectors punish plain writing

**Liang et al.**, "GPT detectors are biased against non-native English
writers," *Patterns* 4(7), 2023, arXiv 2304.02819. [P] (two tracks)

- Seven detectors misclassified TOEFL essays by non-native writers 61.22% of
  the time on average; 89 of 91 were flagged by at least one detector. US
  8th-grade essays were scored almost perfectly.
- Prompting US essays to "Simplify word choices as if written by a non-native
  speaker" raised false positives from 5.19% to 56.65%.
- Prompting AI essays to "Elevate the provided text by employing literary
  language" dropped detection from 100% to 13%.
- [R] "61% of essays flagged" misreads the mean false-positive rate across
  seven detectors. All detectors tested were 2023 versions.
- **So:** plain writing can look *more* like AI to perplexity-based detectors
  (perplexity measures how predictable the wording is to a language model).
  The target is the human reader, not a detector score.

### 6e. AI style is leaking into human writing and speech

- [P] **Yakura et al.**, arXiv 2409.01754 (v4, Jul 2026, preprint): across
  737,083 hours of podcasts, "delve" in unscripted science and tech shows ran
  44% above its expected level 13-18 months after ChatGPT's release. Then
  "The elevation did not persist": it fell below baseline (-35% in the
  general sample), as people learned it marked AI. "Boast," "meticulous" and
  "showcase" kept rising. In a lab test, people picked up a chatbot's words
  after one short chat, and only 3% noticed.
  - [R] Coverage from 2024-25 reports only the rise; the reversal is new in
    v4.
- [P] **Geng & Trotta**, arXiv 2502.09606: "delve" and "intricate" started
  falling in arXiv abstracts in March-April 2024, right after the Liang papers
  named them.
- [P] **Kousha & Thelwall**, arXiv 2509.09596 (abstract only): in PubMed
  Central the words were still climbing through 2025 (delve +1,500% from 2022
  to 2024). The decline is field-specific.
- [P] **Kosch et al.**, arXiv 2609.23090 (abstract): 14,262 CHI papers,
  1982-2026; "prose did not homogenize," and the shift in style began before
  LLMs: "LLMs did not start the change but accelerated it."

## 7. Remedies: what the style guides already said

Every major plain-writing guide maps onto a tell.

**Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946)** [P\*]

| Rule (verbatim) | Tell it counters |
|---|---|
| "Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print." | Stock metaphors, mannered prose |
| "Never use a long word where a short one will do." | "utilize," "facilitate," "leverage" |
| "If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out." | Padding, hedges, closing summaries |
| "Never use the passive where you can use the active." | Agentless corporate passive |
| "Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent." | Unglossed jargon, invented labels |
| "Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous." | Over-correction (§9) |

His four questions: "What am I trying to say? What words will express it?
What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to have
an effect?" His description of bad prose, "phrases tacked together like the
sections of a prefabricated hen-house," fits model defaults closely.

**US Federal Plain Language Guidelines** (Rev. 1, May 2011) [P]

- plainlanguage.gov now redirects to digital.gov; the Center for Plain
  Language reposted the last version as a PDF (see frontmatter). Cite that,
  not plainlanguage.gov.
- "Express only one idea in each sentence."
- "More than any other single technique, using 'you' pulls users into your
  document and makes it relevant to them."
- "Readers complain about jargon more than any other writing fault, because
  writers often fail to realize that terms they know well may be difficult or
  meaningless to their audience."
- On definitions: "use them rarely... It's better to take the time to rewrite
  to avoid needing to define a term."
- "Good examples can substitute for long explanations."
- "Use transition words" (p. 64). Transitions in general are fine; only a
  few specific ones are AI-overused.
- digital.gov [P\*]: "A hidden verb (or nominalization) is a verb converted
  into a noun"; paragraphs of "no more than 150 words in three to eight
  sentences."

**GOV.UK content guidance** [P]

- "Try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long."
- Experts want plain English too: "the more educated the person and the more
  specialist their knowledge, the greater their preference for plain
  English." Relevant to a room of bank engineers.
- "Where you need to use specialist terms, you can. They're not always
  jargon. You just need to explain what they mean the first time you use
  them."
- The A to Z "words to avoid" list pairs each word with a replacement, and
  overlaps heavily with AI vocabulary: deliver (use make, create, provide),
  empower (use allow), facilitate (say what you are doing), foster (use
  encourage or help, unless it's children), leverage (use influence or use,
  unless financial), key (unless it unlocks something).
- Tone: "Write conversationally"; "brisk, but not terse"; avoid adjectives
  that make the text "sound more emotive and like spin."

**Pinker** [P\*]

- Classic style: "The writer can see something that the reader has not yet
  noticed, and he orients the reader so she can see for herself." Counters
  signposting ("This section discusses...") and hedging.
- Curse of knowledge: "a difficulty in imagining what it is like for someone
  else not to know something that you know"; "the chief contributor to
  opaque writing." Remedy: show a draft to real readers.
- "an explanation without an example is little better than no explanation
  at all."
- Book text not fetched; quotes come from his 2014 Chronicle essay and an
  APS report of a lecture.

**Paul Graham, "Write Simply" (2021)** [P\*]: "When you write in a fancy way
to impress people, you're making them do extra work just so you can seem
cool."

**Editors on AI drafts specifically**

- [P\*] Charlie Guo, "The Field Guide to AI Slop" (Oct 2025): the defence
  "isn't to worry too much about style and structure... It's to cultivate
  specificity: to write things rooted in particular knowledge and tangible
  experience." Devices are fine "sparingly, and with intention."
- [P\*] Toastmasters (Apr 2026): AI drafts strip out "your personal insight
  and experiences" and smooth over drama and surprise; "Speakers must
  research each and every fact." One speaker found AI-quoted survey answers
  did not match what respondents wrote.
- [S] HBR "workslop" study (Sep 2025): AI output "that masquerades as good
  work but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance a given task"; 40% of
  1,150 US employees received some in the past month.

**Slides** [P]: Garner & Alley (IJEE 29(6), 2013, 110 students) found
assertion-evidence slides (a full-sentence claim as the headline, a visual as
the evidence) beat topic headings plus bullets on comprehension,
misconceptions, cognitive load and recall at a delayed post-test. This counters the AI
habit of bullet fragments under topic labels.

## 8. Remedies: prompting for style

### 8a. What the vendors say

**Anthropic, "Prompting best practices"** [P] (two tracks)

- "Tell Claude what to do instead of what not to do. Instead of: 'Do not use
  markdown in your response'. Try: 'Your response should be composed of
  smoothly flowing prose paragraphs.'"
- "removing markdown from your prompt can reduce the volume of markdown in
  the output." Write the prompt in the style you want back.
- "Examples are one of the most reliable ways to steer Claude's output
  format, tone, and structure." Use 3-5, varied, in `<example>` tags.
- Give the reason: "Claude can generalize from the explanation."
- The sample anti-markdown block comes with a caveat: Fable 5.1 "already
  formats less than earlier models, so on that model a block like this can
  suppress structure the content needs."

**Anthropic, "Prompting Claude Opus 5"** [P]

- Default answers "run longer than prior Opus models'."
- "Positive examples of the communication style you want tend to be more
  effective than instructions about what not to do."
- "do not pad with filler sections, redundant summaries, or boilerplate."
- On naming things to avoid: "Instructions that call out thinking tags by
  name are less effective than the general form." This is about tag leakage,
  not prose, so it is only an analogy for banned-word lists.

**Anthropic, "Prompting Claude Fable 5.1"** [P] (two tracks). The definition
of mannered prose, which works on any model:

> "Mannered prose substitutes metaphor and flourish for direct statement.
> Instead of 'a parameter worth varying,' the mannered writer produces 'a dial
> worth turning.' Instead of 'this point still matters,' they write 'this point
> earns its keep.' The phrases exist to display the writer, not to convey the
> idea, and readers can tell... The fix is to say what you mean. When a
> literal phrase is available, use it."

"The short version also tends to work: Please remove all mannered prose."

**OpenAI, "Using GPT-6"** [P] (the prompt-guidance page now serves GPT-6;
search results still label it a GPT-5.5 guide)

- GPT-6 Astra "tends to use lists, tables and Markdown to make responses
  scannable."
- "Default to using clear, concise paragraphs, each developing one main idea.
  Use lists only when the information is genuinely parallel, sequential, or
  easier to compare."
- "Use plain language over jargon... calibrate your writing to the level of
  background knowledge assumed from the user's prompt and context."
- Its banned list: "Avoid using slop words or phrases like 'Bottom Line:' in
  conclusions, 'delve,' 'foster,' 'leverage,' 'it's worth noting,'
  'importantly,' 'Question? Answer.' or 'This isn't about X. It's about Y.',
  'genuinely' or hyphenated compound descriptions and adjectives. Do not use
  concluding summary statements such as 'In short:..', 'The simplest mental
  model is:..'... Do not use contrastive framing such as 'X, not Y' that
  introduces an unprompted alternative."

**OpenAI, GPT-5 guide** [P]: style rules fade over a long conversation; the
fix is "appending a Markdown instruction every 3-5 user messages."
Contradictory instructions cost a reasoning model more than vague ones.

### 8b. Where they disagree

- Both vendors: state the target positively, give examples, explain why, and
  make rules conditional ("use lists only when...") rather than absolute.
- OpenAI ships a banned-phrase list. Anthropic prefers defining a category
  with bad and good pairs (mannered prose) and warns that naming a specific
  thing can backfire.
- [P] Older research: language models did worse on negated prompts as they
  scaled (Jang et al. 2022, OPT and GPT-3 era); avoiding a named topic needed
  fine-tuning to beat prompting (Castricato et al. 2024); token banning
  "becomes unusable at just 2,000" patterns (Antislop).
- **Unverified:** no controlled study found showing that banned-word lists
  make current models swap in synonyms. Treat that as folk wisdom. §3a's
  cluster finding (Galpin et al.) makes it plausible.

## 9. Over-correcting

- [P\*] Students now avoid dashes "because professors will think she's using
  AI" (Dreyer). Guo: "Each iteration narrows the space of 'authentic human
  writing' a little more."
- [P\*] Adding typos is bad advice: "Both typos and grammos had a negative
  impact on the evaluation scale" (Boland & Queen, PLOS ONE 2016). Writers of
  error-filled emails were seen as "less conscientious, intelligent, and
  trustworthy" (Vignovic & Thompson 2010, [S]).
- [P] Informality alone does not work: one of the five experts was fooled by
  contractions, the other four were not, and o1-pro already writes
  contractions (Russell et al.).
- [S] "Humanizer" tools have been reported to change facts and add grammar
  errors (Copyleaks, a detector vendor, so conflicted). A "Stanford HAI 12%
  factual errors" figure circulates without a primary source [R]; do not
  cite it.
- [O] Do not strip every triad, transition or dash. The tell is frequency and
  sameness, not the device.

## 10. Jargon for a lay audience

- [P] Shulman et al., *J. Language & Social Psychology* 2020: jargon
  "disrupts people's ability to fluently process scientific information, even
  when definitions for the jargon terms are provided," and lowers interest and
  sense of belonging. Shulman [P\*]: "giving people definitions didn't matter
  at all."
- [P] Bullock et al., *Public Understanding of Science* 2019: jargon leads
  to "increased risk perceptions, and lower support for technology adoption."
  Relevant to any deck asking colleagues to try AI tools.
- [P\*] Bullock, Shulman et al., PLOS ONE 2020: jargon had no effect when the
  topic was urgent (COVID-19); otherwise it hurt. Motivated audiences
  tolerate more.
- [P\*] Rakedzon et al., PLOS ONE 2017: the benchmark is about 2% unfamiliar
  words, from reading research showing comprehension needs about 98% familiar
  words (Hu & Nation 2000, [S]).
- [E] Analogies: thin evidence. Analogies "included superficially and
  without explanation" can create an illusion of understanding (Griffin et
  al., preprint).
- **Order of preference [O]:** cut the term; rewrite around it; if it must
  stay, explain it on first use with an example rather than a dictionary
  definition. Keep surviving terms to a handful per deck.

## 11. Revision checklist for deck text and speaker notes [O]

Built from §3-§10. Run it on every audience-facing draft.

1. **Lead with the point.** Slide titles are full-sentence claims; each notes
   paragraph opens with its point. (OpenAI GPT-6; Garner & Alley)
2. **Keep a list only if the items are truly parallel or sequential.**
   Otherwise use a sentence, a number, a chart or an example. (OpenAI; Alley;
   Pangram bullet ratio)
3. **One specific per claim.** A real number, system, date, name or episode
   from our own work. If none exists, cut the claim. (Guo; Russell
   originality; Pangram on proper nouns)
4. **Delete the closing summary, the moral and the upbeat wrap-up.** End when
   the content ends. (Russell conclusions; Wikipedia; OpenAI; Opus 5 docs)
5. **Cut announcements of importance.** "This matters," "why X matters,"
   "the key insight," "here's the thing." Show why it matters instead.
   (Graphite on Opus 5/5.5)
6. **Cut "not X, it's Y" and "rather than" when nobody claimed X.**
   (Wikipedia; Graphite; OpenAI)
7. **Turn trailing -ing clauses into their own sentence with a subject and
   verb.** "..., highlighting the risk" becomes "That is a risk because...".
   (Reinhart; Wikipedia)
8. **Turn hidden nouns back into verbs, and name who acts.** "Implementation
   of controls" becomes "we added controls". (digital.gov; Orwell; Pinker)
9. **Swap AI and buzz vocabulary for the plain word,** using GOV.UK's pairs
   (leverage to use, facilitate to say what you did, foster to help). Treat
   §3a and §5 as a list to check against, not a ban list.
10. **Literal over mannered.** Replace stock metaphors ("load-bearing,"
    "earns its keep," "a dial worth turning") with what you mean. Keep an
    analogy only if it is fresh and explained. (Fable 5.1 docs; Orwell)
11. **Vary rhythm.** Split sentences over about 25 words, but keep some long
    ones; no runs of one-line fragments. Read the notes aloud. (GOV.UK;
    Pangram on uniformity; Kay's feedback)
12. **One hedge per real uncertainty, stated once.** (Pinker; Opus 5 docs)
13. **Jargon pass** per §10. (Shulman; federal guidelines; Rakedzon)
14. **Check every fact and quote against the source.** Never let an edit
    replace a vague claim with an invented number. (Toastmasters;
    `research/` as the receipt)
15. **Don't over-correct.** Keep a deliberate dash, triad or transition if it
    helps. Never add errors. Ignore detector scores. (§9; Orwell vi)

## 12. Prompting recipe when drafting with Claude [O]

- Describe the target positively, with the reason: "Write in plain spoken
  English for bank engineers who are new to AI. Short paragraphs, one idea
  each. Explain any AI term the first time with an everyday example."
- Write the prompt itself in that style (no bullets and bold if you want
  none back).
- Paste 2-3 paragraphs of Kay's own approved writing as examples.
- Name anti-patterns as defined categories with bad/good pairs, such as
  Anthropic's mannered-prose paragraph, rather than bare banned words.
- Add the model-specific line when drafting with Opus 5 or 5.5: "Don't
  announce that something matters or rebut claims nobody made; show the
  reason instead."
- Restate the style rules every few turns in a long session.
- Then run §11 by hand. Prompting reduces tells; it does not remove them.
